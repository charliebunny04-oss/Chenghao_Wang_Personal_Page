How Much Cross-Sector Excitation Survives VIX Conditioning?

A VIX-augmented daily self-exciting model for FF12 Money and BusEq negative-tail events.

Research question

Separating observed stress from apparent event-history dependence.

Negative-tail events can cluster across sectors because one sector predicts another, because both respond to persistent market stress, or because own-sector history and unobserved shocks remain. The study asks a deliberately bounded attribution question rather than claiming causal contagion.

Why it matters

If a constant background omits an observable common-stress state, event-history terms may absorb some clustering associated with market-wide stress. Risk monitoring based on those terms needs to distinguish model-based excitation from broader stress exposure.

Literature gap

Prior work separately shows that implied volatility can improve extreme-return point-process forecasts, that background misspecification can inflate apparent excitation, and that daily tails can use discrete self-exciting formulations. Direct evidence on VIX-conditioned directional attenuation between two FF12 portfolios remains limited.

Negative-tail event definition

Each sector uses the fifth percentile of its own training-period returns. Cut-offs are frozen for primary out-of-sample evaluation. If both sectors have events on the same day, both are retained; no intraday ordering is invented.

Main estimands

Directional kernel summaries before and after VIX conditioning.

The focus is the change in Money → BusEq and BusEq → Money cumulative kernel summaries, their total, and dependent-data bootstrap intervals. In this binary-hazard setting they are persistence summaries analogous to Hawkes kernel mass, not automatically expected offspring counts.

Current status & limitations

The design is public; the empirical claims are not yet available.

The latest recorded execution state is preflight failed with no estimation. The CSV import and preflight checks must be corrected before model work resumes.

  • VIX is an observed common-stress proxy, not an instrument or a complete measure of common shocks.
  • Daily data do not reveal within-day event order, so the primary likelihood remains discrete-time.
  • Rare events may produce weak identification, boundary estimates or wide bootstrap intervals.
  • Any eventual result supports conditional attribution and prediction, not causal financial contagion.
